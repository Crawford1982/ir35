What is IR35? A Plain English Guide for UK Contractors (2025/26 & 2026)

IR35 explained from scratch — what it is, how it affects your take-home pay, how your status is assessed, and what's changing in April 2026.

⏱ 12 min read 📅 March 2026 🎯 Covers 2025/26 & 2026/27

What is IR35?

IR35 is UK tax legislation that targets disguised employment — situations where a contractor works through their own limited company but, in reality, is working like an employee of the company they contract for.

The name comes from the Inland Revenue press release that announced it: IR35, published in 1999. It came into force in April 2000 and has been controversial ever since.

The legislation exists because working through a limited company is more tax-efficient than being an employee. A contractor outside IR35 can pay themselves a low salary and take the rest of their income as dividends — dividends aren't subject to National Insurance and are taxed at lower rates than salary. HMRC estimated this was costing billions of pounds a year in lost tax, which is why IR35 was introduced.

IR35 in one sentence

If you work through your own limited company but you're effectively an employee of your client, HMRC wants you to pay the same income tax and National Insurance as an actual employee.

Inside vs Outside IR35: What Does It Mean?

Every contractor who works through a limited company is either inside IR35 or outside IR35 on each contract they hold. It's the single most important factor in how much tax you pay.

Outside IR35

You are genuinely in business for yourself. You can take the income through your limited company, pay yourself a small salary, and take the rest as dividends. This is significantly more tax-efficient.

Inside IR35

HMRC (or your client) considers you to be a deemed employee. Your contract income must be paid to you subject to income tax and employee's National Insurance — as if you were on the payroll. You also lose employer's NI from your gross rate, which you'd otherwise keep. You still don't get employment rights like holiday pay or sick pay.

⚠️ The "inside IR35" trap

Being inside IR35 means you pay employee tax and NI without getting employment benefits. Many contractors inside IR35 effectively earn less than an equivalent permanent employee, once you account for no employer pension contributions, no sick pay, no redundancy rights, and no holiday pay on top of the tax hit.

How is IR35 Status Determined?

IR35 status is determined by looking at the real-world working relationship between you and your client — not the wording of your contract alone (though the contract matters). HMRC and the courts use several tests, but three dominate every assessment. See our detailed guide to the 3 IR35 status tests for a full breakdown of how each is assessed.

🔄 Substitution

Can you send a substitute to do the work if you're unavailable, without the client's approval? A genuine contractor can. An employee cannot.

Outside if: genuine substitution right exists

🎛️ Control

Does the client control how, when, and where you work? Or do they only specify what outcome they want? Employees are controlled; genuine contractors are not.

Outside if: output-based, not hours-based

🤝 Mutuality of Obligation

Is the client obliged to offer you work, and are you obliged to accept it? True contractors have no ongoing obligation either way once a contract ends.

Outside if: no obligation after contract ends

💼 In Business for Yourself

Do you have multiple clients, bear financial risk, provide your own equipment, and operate with a business-like structure? These support outside IR35.

Outside if: genuine business indicators present
🚫 Don't rely solely on HMRC's CEST tool

HMRC's Check Employment Status for Tax (CEST) tool returns "undetermined" for around 1 in 5 assessments and completely ignores Mutuality of Obligation — the most-litigated test in IR35 case law. Use CEST as a starting point only. A professional IR35 contract review from a specialist costs £100–£300 and is money well spent. See our guide: Why HMRC's CEST Tool Gets IR35 Wrong →

Who Decides Your IR35 Status?

This is where many contractors get confused. The answer depends on the size of the company you're contracting for.

Client Type Who Assesses IR35? Legal Framework
Public sector (NHS, councils, government, etc.) The client (end engager) Off-payroll working rules (Chapter 10, ITEPA 2003) — since April 2017
Medium or large private sector company The client (end engager) Off-payroll working rules (Chapter 10, ITEPA 2003) — since April 2021
Small private sector company You, the contractor Original IR35 rules (Chapter 8, ITEPA 2003)

What counts as a "small" company?

A company qualifies as small if it meets at least two of these three criteria:

  • Annual turnover of £10.2 million or less (rising to £15 million from 6 April 2026)
  • Balance sheet total of £5.1 million or less (rising to £7.5 million from 6 April 2026)
  • Fewer than 50 employees

When contracting for a small company, you are responsible for self-assessing your IR35 status and paying any additional tax owed if you are inside IR35. You can ask your client to confirm their size in writing — they have 45 days to respond under ESM10011A.

✅ April 2026: More clients will classify as small

From 6 April 2026, the turnover threshold rises from £10.2m to £15m and the balance sheet threshold rises from £5.1m to £7.5m. HMRC estimates around 14,000 companies will reclassify as small — meaning IR35 responsibility reverts back to the contractor for those engagements. This is potentially good news for contractors who are genuinely outside IR35 and have been caught by blanket inside determinations from medium-sized clients. Full guide to the April 2026 changes →

A Brief History of IR35

Understanding how IR35 evolved helps make sense of the current rules.

00
April 2000
IR35 introduced. Contractors operating through limited companies must self-assess whether they would be employees if working directly. If yes, they pay income tax and NI on their income. Widely criticised and largely ignored — compliance was low and HMRC struggled to enforce it contractor by contractor.
17
April 2017
Public sector reform. For contractors working in the public sector, responsibility for determining IR35 status shifts from the contractor to the public sector client. Banks, NHS trusts, local authorities and government departments must now assess every contractor engagement and issue a Status Determination Statement (SDS). Many public sector clients issue blanket "inside" determinations to reduce their own risk.
21
April 2021
Private sector reform (off-payroll working rules). The same rules extended to medium and large private sector companies, after a one-year delay due to COVID. This was the biggest change to UK contractor taxation in 20 years. Many large businesses issued blanket inside determinations rather than assess engagements individually, and significant numbers of contractors were pushed into umbrella companies or left contracting entirely.
26
April 2026
Small company threshold rises. The definition of "small" expands, meaning more clients reclassify as small companies and IR35 responsibility reverts to contractors for those engagements. Simultaneously, umbrella company PAYE responsibility shifts from umbrella companies to recruitment agencies. Both changes take effect from 6 April 2026.

Does IR35 Apply to Sole Traders?

No. IR35 only applies to contractors who work through an intermediary — most commonly a personal service company (PSC) or limited company. If you are a sole trader, you pay income tax and National Insurance through Self Assessment directly on your profits. There is no intermediary structure for HMRC to challenge, so IR35 is irrelevant to you.

However, sole traders lose the ability to take income as dividends, claim limited company expenses, or benefit from the corporation tax rate on retained profits. Many contractors who are genuinely in business for themselves still prefer the limited company structure for these tax efficiencies — as long as they are outside IR35.

What Should I Do?

Your action checklist depends on your situation:

📋 If you're a contractor starting or reviewing a contract
  • Find out whether your client is small, medium or large — ask for written confirmation of their size if in doubt
  • If they're medium/large (or public sector), get their Status Determination Statement (SDS) in writing before you start
  • Have your contract reviewed by an IR35 specialist — not just HMRC's CEST tool. See our IR35 contract review checklist
  • Ensure your working practices reflect your contract (don't say "right to substitute" in the contract if you'd never actually use it)
  • Consider IR35 insurance — it covers investigation costs and any tax owed. Read about what happens in an HMRC IR35 investigation
📋 If your client is reclassifying as small in April 2026
  • IR35 responsibility reverts to you — you will need to self-assess your status for the new tax year
  • Your client is no longer legally required to issue an SDS, but it's still good practice to discuss status with them
  • If you believe you are outside IR35, get a professional contract review to document your reasoning
  • If you've been inside IR35 under their previous determination and genuinely believe you're outside, this is your opportunity — but get the working practices right, not just the contract wording

Frequently Asked Questions

Does being inside IR35 mean I'm employed?
No. Inside IR35 means you are taxed as if you were employed — you pay the same income tax and National Insurance as an employee. But you are not actually employed and have none of the rights that go with employment: no unfair dismissal protection, no statutory sick pay, no holiday pay entitlement, no employer pension contributions. This is why many contractors view inside IR35 as the worst of both worlds.
Can I challenge my IR35 determination if I disagree with it?
Yes. If a medium or large client issues a Status Determination Statement you disagree with, you can use the formal disagreement process — the client must consider your representations and respond within 45 days. If they don't respond, they take on any tax liability. Beyond the client's internal process, you can escalate to HMRC or ultimately challenge through the courts, but this is expensive and time-consuming. Some contractors choose to leave the engagement instead of accepting an incorrect inside determination.
What is a Status Determination Statement (SDS)?
An SDS is a written determination of your IR35 status, issued by your client (for medium/large clients and public sector). It must state whether your engagement is inside or outside IR35 and give reasons for the conclusion. Clients who don't issue an SDS before the engagement starts take on any tax liability if HMRC later disagrees. You should always ask for your SDS in writing and keep it on file.
If I'm inside IR35, can I still work through my limited company?
Technically yes, but your income from that contract must be treated as a "deemed employment payment" — processed through PAYE by whoever is paying you (the agency or client). Your limited company receives the payment net of the employer's NI deducted at source. You can still use your limited company for outside IR35 contracts simultaneously, and you can still take a salary from your company. But you lose the dividend advantage on the inside IR35 income.
What is IR35 insurance and do I need it?
IR35 insurance (often bundled with Tax Enquiry insurance) covers the cost of defending an HMRC investigation into your IR35 status — accountant and legal fees can run to tens of thousands of pounds — and in some policies, the tax liability itself if HMRC wins. Contractors who are outside IR35 and have documented their status carefully should consider it. Policies typically cost £200–£400 per year through professional contractor bodies such as IPSE or directly through insurers. It is not relevant if you are already inside IR35 and paying the correct tax.
What's the difference between IR35 and the off-payroll working rules?
"IR35" is the colloquial name for two related sets of rules. The original IR35 (Chapter 8 of ITEPA 2003) applies when contractors self-assess their status — currently relevant for small company engagements. The off-payroll working rules (Chapter 10 of ITEPA 2003) apply when medium/large clients or public sector bodies make the determination. Both achieve the same result — taxing disguised employees correctly — but via different mechanisms. When people talk about "IR35" in everyday contractor conversation, they usually mean whichever rules apply to their current situation.
